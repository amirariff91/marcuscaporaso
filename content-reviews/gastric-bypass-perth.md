## Must fix before publish

1. **Medicare item-number wording is not accurate enough for a Roux-en-Y page**

> Medicare item numbers 31572 and 31581 attract a rebate when eligibility criteria are met, and private health insurance (typically a gold-tier or equivalent policy) covers the hospital and theatre component.

> Medicare rebates may apply when MBS criteria are met. For Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, the relevant MBS item is 31572; your surgeon will confirm the appropriate Medicare item number, rebate eligibility and any private health fund requirements before surgery. Private health insurance with appropriate hospital cover may contribute to hospital and theatre costs, but out-of-pocket costs vary by fund and policy.

Why it matters: Item 31572 is the Roux-en-Y gastric bypass item, while 31581 is for biliopancreatic diversion with or without duodenal switch, so listing both as if they apply to this page is a compliance risk.

2. **BMI eligibility is broader than current MBS wording [also fix the brief]**

> A gastric bypass is generally considered suitable for adults with a BMI of 35 or higher, or a BMI of 30-35 with associated weight-related health conditions such as type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure, sleep apnoea, or reflux.

> Gastric bypass suitability is assessed individually. For Medicare-funded bariatric surgery, clinically severe obesity is generally described as a BMI of 40 or higher, or a BMI of 35 or higher with major medical comorbidities, although your surgeon will consider your full health picture and whether any population-specific or clinical factors apply. Some lower BMI ranges may be discussed for selected metabolic-surgery patients, but Medicare rebate eligibility must be confirmed by the surgical team.

Why it matters: MBS explanatory note TN.8.29 generally describes clinically severe obesity as BMI 40 or higher, or BMI 35 or higher with major medical comorbidities; the writer followed the brief as instructed, so the brief also needs correction.

3. **"Generally safe" is an unbalanced safety claim**

> Gastric bypass surgery is well-established and generally safe, but it is still a major surgery which carries with it some real risks, all of which your surgeon will discuss with you in detail.

> Gastric bypass surgery is a major operation, and every patient should understand the potential benefits, limits and risks before deciding. Serious complications are uncommon but can occur, including bleeding, infection, clots, anaesthetic complications, leaks, internal hernia, dumping syndrome and long-term vitamin or mineral deficiency. Your surgeon will explain how these risks apply to your health before you proceed.

Why it matters: "Generally safe" can underplay the seriousness of the operation unless the sentence gives a fuller risk picture in the same breath.

4. **The diabetes medication claim misses the required doctor-review qualifier**

> Strong improvement in type 2 diabetes in many patients, with some being able to reduce or stop medication; outcomes vary between individuals.

> Type 2 diabetes can improve after gastric bypass for some patients, and some may be able to reduce medication after review with their GP, endocrinologist or surgeon. Outcomes vary, and gastric bypass should not be described as a cure for diabetes.

Why it matters: Medication changes should be framed as something that happens after review with a treating doctor, not as a standalone surgery benefit.

5. **The comparison copy uses a broad "better outcomes" claim**

> Compared to a gastric sleeve, which simply restricts the flow of food through your digestive system, the bypass often results in greater weight loss and better outcomes, especially for those living with type 2 diabetes and acid reflux.

> Compared with a gastric sleeve, gastric bypass may produce slightly greater average excess weight loss and may be preferred for some patients with type 2 diabetes or significant reflux, but it is a more complex operation with lifelong nutritional requirements. The best option depends on your medical history, goals and risk profile.

Why it matters: "Better outcomes" is too broad for health advertising unless the claim is balanced with complexity, risk and individual suitability.
