# Gastric Sleeve Perth — content review

**Verdict: Rework** · 7 must-fix · 11 should-fix · 4 minor

**Why:** Safety, Medicare eligibility, hospital verification, cost certainty and outcome-claim issues need correction before publication.

**Length:** current body 1,844 words vs brief target 1,200-1,500 words, including FAQs.

## Must fix before publish

1. **AHPRA risk: the opening claim overstates safety and effectiveness**

> Yet in the right hands, gastric sleeve surgery is a safe, powerful, and lasting weight loss tool.

> For some people, gastric sleeve surgery can support substantial long-term weight loss, but it is major surgery with risks, and suitability needs to be assessed by a bariatric surgeon.

Why it matters: "Safe, powerful, and lasting" creates an unbalanced beneficial expectation for a regulated health service, so risk balance needs to sit in the same sentence.

2. **MBS/BMI eligibility is not accurate enough for Medicare item 31575 [also fix the brief]**

> A sleeve gastrectomy is generally considered suitable for adults with a BMI of 35 or higher, or a BMI of 30-35 with associated weight-related health conditions such as type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure, sleep apnoea, or reflux.

> For Medicare item 31575, clinically severe obesity is generally described as a BMI of 40 or higher, or a BMI of 35 or higher with major medical co-morbidities such as diabetes or cardiovascular disease; suitability for surgery is still assessed individually by the surgeon, and BMI thresholds may vary for some population groups.

Why it matters: The draft's BMI 30-35 framing may be a broader clinical eligibility statement, but it should not sit unqualified on a page that also discusses Medicare item 31575; the writer followed the brief as instructed, so the brief also needs correction.

3. **Hospital names must be verified with the client before changing either direction**

> You'll spend 1-2 nights recuperating at [<u>Mount Private Hospital</u>](https://mountprivatehospital.com.au), [<u>The Park Private Hospital</u>](https://www.tpph.com.au/), or [<u>Waikiki Private Hospital</u>](https://waikikiprivate.com.au/) in Perth.

> Most patients spend around two nights in hospital at the hospital location confirmed by their surgeon, with discharge timing depending on recovery and clinical advice.

Why it matters: The hospital names conflict with the brief, but the draft may reflect newer client information, so verify the correct hospital name(s) with the client before changing either direction.

4. **The cost paragraph breaches the brief by quoting a single dollar figure**

> Your surgeon's fee for a primary laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy is $4,000 for both insured and uninsured patients, rising to $5,000 for a complex case.

> Total out-of-pocket costs vary depending on your private health cover, surgeon and anaesthetist fees, hospital charges, complexity and any applicable gap, so the safest next step is to review the current gastric sleeve cost guide before deciding whether to proceed.

Why it matters: The brief explicitly says not to quote a single dollar figure, and any pricing needs complete, current and approved fee disclosure.

5. **Appetite wording is too certain**

> Secondly, because a reduced stomach size minimises your body's production of ghrelin (the 'hunger hormone'), after gastric sleeve surgery, your appetite will also be reduced.

> The operation also removes part of the stomach that produces much of the body's ghrelin, a hormone involved in hunger, so many people notice reduced appetite after surgery; the degree and duration vary.

Why it matters: "Will also be reduced" reads like an outcome guarantee, while the rewrite keeps the mechanism and qualifies individual variation.

6. **The "life-changing" phrase creates a transformational-benefit claim**

> While it can be life-changing, weight loss surgery is a tool, not a one-off fix.

> Weight loss surgery is a tool that works alongside long-term changes, not a one-off fix.

Why it matters: "Life-changing" risks creating an unreasonable expectation of benefit under health advertising rules, even when the surrounding sentence is otherwise balanced.

7. **Private-health coverage claims are too absolute**

> Private health insurance (typically a gold-tier or equivalent policy) covers the hospital and theatre component.

> Private health insurance with appropriate hospital cover, typically gold tier, may contribute to hospital and theatre costs; out-of-pockets vary by fund, policy, waiting periods and gaps.

Why it matters: Coverage depends on fund rules, policy settings, waiting periods and gap arrangements, so the copy should not imply automatic cover.

## Should fix

1. **Primary keyword placement is weak in the H1 and first 100 words**

> If you are one of the [<u>75% of West Australians</u>](https://www.health.wa.gov.au/~/media/Corp/Documents/Health-for/Nutrition-monitoring-survey-series/2022/Weight-and-Health_FINAL.pdf) living with overweight or obesity and are exploring different types of bariatric surgery, you may be considering a sleeve gastrectomy (gastric sleeve surgery).

> If you are researching gastric sleeve Perth options after years of weight loss and regain, this guide explains the procedure, costs, Medicare considerations, risks, recovery and how OSWA assesses whether surgery is suitable for you.

Why it matters: Use the H1 "Gastric Sleeve Perth: Surgery, Cost, Eligibility and Recovery" and a title tag such as "Gastric Sleeve Perth: Cost & Recovery | OSWA" so the exact primary keyword appears in the title, H1 and first 100 words.

2. **The draft is over the 1,200-1,500 word target**

> Beyond weight loss alone, gastric sleeve surgery offers a range of benefits that can improve both your physical health and day-to-day quality of life.

> For suitable patients, gastric sleeve surgery may support weight loss and improvement in some weight-related conditions, but benefits and risks need to be weighed with your surgeon.

Why it matters: The draft is 1,844 words including FAQs, so cut duplicate benefit phrasing, shorten the cost paragraph, merge benefits and risks into tighter bullets, and keep FAQ answers concise.

3. **Current Google People-Also-Ask questions checked 10 Jul 2026 ask for sleeve-vs-medication comparison**

> A gastric sleeve reduces stomach size but leaves the digestive pathway unchanged.

> A gastric sleeve permanently reduces stomach size, a gastric bypass reduces stomach size and reroutes part of the small intestine, and prescription weight-management medicines work differently again; there is no single best option for everyone, and your surgeon can explain how reflux, BMI, diabetes, medication history and surgical risk affect the choice.

Why it matters: This answers current search demand without using prescription brand names such as Ozempic, Wegovy or Mounjaro in promotional copy.

4. **Current Google People-Also-Ask questions checked 10 Jul 2026 ask who is not eligible**

> Your BMI is a great starting point, however your surgeon will take the time to assess your whole health picture to determine if you are a good candidate for a gastric sleeve.

> BMI is only a starting point; your surgeon will also assess your medical history, surgical and anaesthetic risk, nutrition, reflux, mental health, previous weight-loss attempts and ability to commit to follow-up, and some people may need conditions managed first or may not be suitable for surgery.

Why it matters: Keep this in the eligibility section and link to the dedicated eligibility page with the anchor text "am I eligible for weight loss surgery".

5. **Current Google People-Also-Ask questions checked 10 Jul 2026 ask about the 30/30 eating rule**

> By weeks two to three, your diet progresses from fluids to pureed and soft foods.

> By weeks two to three, your diet usually progresses from fluids to pureed and soft foods, and your dietitian will teach eating habits such as separating food and fluids if that is part of your post-op plan.

Why it matters: This covers the search intent without presenting a universal rule as medical advice.

6. **Current Google People-Also-Ask questions checked 10 Jul 2026 ask about longer-term outcomes**

> Some weight regain over time is not uncommon and long-term success is generally supported by ongoing follow-up with your surgical and dietetic team.

> Long-term results vary: many people maintain meaningful weight loss, but some regain can occur over the years, especially if eating habits, activity, medical issues or follow-up care change.

Why it matters: This addresses "what happens 10 years after gastric sleeve" without adding a long new FAQ.

7. **E-E-A-T signals are missing from the draft**

> Your surgeon will discuss long-term expectations with you during your consultation.

> Medically reviewed by Dr Harsha Chandraratna, MBBS, FRACS, Bariatric & Upper GI Surgeon, and Dr Stephen Watson, MBBS, FRACS, Bariatric Surgeon; last updated July 2026.

Why it matters: Add a visible reviewer line near the top or bottom, plus citations for WA Health, MBS item 31575 and any clinical averages used.

8. **Surgeon credentials required by the brief are absent**

> The best way to explore your options is to schedule an initial consultation with a Perth bariatric surgeon.

> The best way to explore your options is to book an initial consultation with an OSWA bariatric surgeon, where Dr Harsha Chandraratna or Dr Stephen Watson can assess your health history, goals and suitability.

Why it matters: Link "OSWA bariatric surgeon" to `/surgeons` and "book an initial consultation" to `/book-consultation`.

9. **Internal-link anchor text is too vague**

> A full breakdown of costs, funding pathways, and pre-surgical clinical fees can be found here.

> Read our gastric sleeve cost Perth guide for itemised cost components, Medicare item 31575, private health cover and funding options.

Why it matters: Link "gastric sleeve cost Perth guide" to `/gastric-sleeve-cost-perth` and "private health cover" to `/medicare-private-health-cover`.

10. **The bypass comparison overstates malabsorption differences**

> No intestinal rerouting; food travels through the digestive tract in its normal order, meaning no malabsorption as is seen with a bypass.

> No intestinal rerouting; food travels through the digestive tract in its normal order, so the sleeve is less malabsorptive than a bypass, although vitamin and mineral monitoring is still needed.

Why it matters: The draft later discusses B12, iron and vitamin D deficiencies, so "no malabsorption" reads too absolute.

11. **The CTA implies a transformational outcome**

> Ready to explore whether a gastric sleeve could change things for you?

> Ready to discuss whether a gastric sleeve is clinically appropriate for you?

Why it matters: A consultation CTA should frame the next step as clinical assessment, not an expected personal transformation.

## Minor

1. **The gastric band comparison distracts from the sleeve-vs-bypass intent**

> Food still travels and is digested as it always has, just more slowly.

> For most readers comparing common options, the key distinction is that a sleeve reduces stomach size while a bypass also reroutes part of the small intestine; the right choice depends on reflux history, BMI, weight-related conditions and surgeon assessment.

Why it matters: The target comparison keyword is "gastric sleeve vs bypass", not sleeve vs band.

2. **The BMI prompt should be turned into a useful internal link**

> Calculate your BMI.

> Use our eligibility guide to understand how BMI, weight-related conditions and surgical risk are assessed.

Why it matters: Point "eligibility guide" to `/am-i-eligible` so the prompt moves readers to the right support page.

3. **The recovery wording can be made less prescriptive**

> You'll be prescribed ~2 weeks' rest from desk work, however physical or manual labour will require closer to 4-6 weeks.

> Many people are advised to allow around two weeks before returning to desk work, while physical or manual work may require closer to four to six weeks depending on recovery and surgeon advice.

Why it matters: "You'll be prescribed" and "will require" are more certain than necessary for a general procedure page.

4. **The cost paragraph should avoid credit-style language in the main body**

> Financing options such as Zip and TLC are also available for eligible patients.

> Some patients explore approved payment or funding pathways, but these should be discussed separately and should not influence whether surgery is clinically appropriate.

Why it matters: Financing can look like an inducement on a health-service page, so if retained it should sit in the cost guide with current terms and compliance review.
