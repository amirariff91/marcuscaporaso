### What is a gastric sleeve?
- ~3 paragraphs
- **Para 1:** Define the procedure. Explain that roughly 75–80% of the stomach is removed, leaving a narrow banana-shaped tube that holds far less food. Use plain language — no jargon.
- **Para 2:** Explain the hormonal mechanism — the removed portion produces most of the body's ghrelin (hunger hormone), so patients experience reduced appetite after surgery, not just smaller capacity. This is the "why it works differently from a diet" paragraph.
- **Para 3:** Be direct about permanence — this is an irreversible change to anatomy. Then distinguish from a bypass: the sleeve does not reroute the intestines, so food still travels through the digestive system in its normal order. Frame this as context for decision-making, not a selling point.

**Comparison note to weave in:** A short paragraph (or callout) comparing sleeve vs bypass — a bypass reroutes the small intestine to reduce calorie absorption; a sleeve does not reroute anything. Neither is universally better. The right choice depends on the patient's weight, health conditions, reflux history and surgeon discussion.

### Eligibility
- **BMI note:** Generally suitable for adults with a BMI of 35 or above, or BMI 30–35 with weight-related health conditions such as type 2 diabetes, sleep apnoea or high blood pressure.
- **Bullet points to address:**
  1. BMI is a starting point only — the surgeon assesses the whole health picture, not just a number.
  2. The reader has tried to lose weight through diet and lifestyle changes without lasting success. Normalise this — it is expected, not a mark against them.
  3. The reader must be prepared to commit to long-term dietary changes and ongoing follow-up. Surgery is a tool, not a one-off fix.
  4. There are no untreated conditions that would make surgery unsafe — some conditions need management first rather than ruling surgery out.
  5. Close with a gentle nudge: the only real way to know whether they qualify is an assessment. An eligibility check is the simple place to begin.

### How it works (6 steps)
Each step gets a short heading and 1–2 sentences of content direction.

1. **Initial consultation and assessment** — The reader meets their surgeon, talks through medical history and goals, and gets an honest view of whether the sleeve suits them. This is also where they can ask the questions that feel awkward to raise.
2. **Pre-surgery preparation** — The patient may need to follow a specific pre-op diet (typically 2–4 weeks) to shrink the liver and reduce surgical risk. Emphasise that the team supports them through this — not just a handout.
3. **The procedure** — General anaesthetic, keyhole technique, 45–60 minutes. Surgeon removes ~75–80% of the stomach and seals the new sleeve with a staple line. Keep it clinical but accessible.
4. **Hospital recovery** — 1–2 nights at St John of God Murdoch or Subiaco. Patient starts on fluids and progresses under guidance from nursing and dietetic staff.
5. **Returning to daily life** — Desk work after ~2 weeks; physical or manual labour closer to 4–6 weeks. Surgeon provides advice tailored to the individual's situation.
6. **Long-term follow-up** — Staged eating progression (fluids → soft → regular textures) with dietitian support. Ongoing reviews and blood tests to protect nutrition and results long-term.

### Benefits (6 bullets)
Each bullet should convey one clear, qualified benefit. Do not use superlatives.

1. Significant, sustained weight loss — clinical average of 60–70% of excess weight over 12–18 months; individual results vary.
2. Reduced appetite — the procedure lowers ghrelin production, so smaller portions feel more satisfying rather than like deprivation.
3. Improvement in weight-related conditions — type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure, sleep apnoea, joint pain. Qualify that outcomes differ between individuals.
4. No intestinal rerouting — food travels through the digestive tract in its normal order; no malabsorption as seen with a bypass.
5. Keyhole approach — smaller scars, generally less post-operative pain, shorter hospital stay than open surgery.
6. Single permanent procedure — no implanted device to adjust or maintain, unlike a gastric band.

### Risks / honest section
- **Intro paragraph tone:** Acknowledge directly that the gastric sleeve is well-established and generally safe, but that it is still major surgery with real risks. The practice would rather the reader hear these plainly now than be surprised later. The surgeon will go through each risk in the context of the individual's health before any decision is made.
- **Risk bullets to address:**
  1. Staple line leak — occurs in roughly 1–3% of cases; the most serious early complication, monitored closely and may require further treatment.
  2. Reflux or heartburn — can be new or worsened after a sleeve; settles for some, persistent for others; occasionally influences the choice of procedure.
  3. Nutritional deficiencies — particularly vitamin B12, iron and vitamin D; lifelong supplements and regular blood tests are part of staying well.
  4. Hair thinning — common in the months after surgery, typically linked to rapid weight loss and nutritional intake; usually temporary and improves as intake stabilises.
  5. Weight regain — possible if eating habits drift and follow-up lapses; the sleeve works best alongside ongoing support.
  6. General surgical risks — bleeding, infection, blood clots and anaesthetic reactions, as with any operation.
