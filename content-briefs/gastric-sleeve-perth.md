### What is a gastric sleeve?
- ~3 paragraphs
- **Para 1:** Define the procedure. Explain that most of the stomach is removed, leaving a narrow banana-shaped tube that holds far less food. Use plain language — no jargon.
- **Para 2:** Explain the hormonal mechanism — the removed portion produces most of the body's ghrelin (hunger hormone), so some patients may experience reduced appetite after surgery, not just smaller capacity. This is the "why it works differently from a diet" paragraph.
- **Para 3:** Be direct about permanence — this is an irreversible change to anatomy. Then distinguish from a bypass: the sleeve does not reroute the intestines, so food still travels through the digestive system in its normal order. Frame this as context for decision-making, not a selling point.

**Comparison note to weave in:** A short paragraph (or callout) comparing sleeve vs bypass — a bypass reroutes the small intestine to reduce calorie absorption; a sleeve does not reroute anything. Neither is universally better. The right choice depends on the patient's weight, health conditions, reflux history and surgeon discussion.

### Eligibility
- **MBS/BMI note:** For Medicare item 31575, eligibility generally requires a BMI of 40 or above, or BMI 35–39.9 with at least one major obesity-related condition — always subject to individual clinical assessment.
- **Bullet points to address:**
  1. BMI is a starting point only — the surgeon assesses the whole health picture, not just a number.
  2. The reader has tried to lose weight through diet and lifestyle changes without lasting success. Normalise this — it is expected, not a mark against them.
  3. The reader must be prepared to commit to long-term dietary changes and ongoing follow-up. Surgery is a tool, not a one-off fix.
  4. Some health conditions may need management before surgery; suitability is assessed individually and no outcome or safety is guaranteed.
  5. Close with a gentle nudge: the only real way to know whether they qualify is an assessment. An eligibility check is the simple place to begin.

3. **The procedure** — General anaesthetic and keyhole technique where clinically appropriate. The surgeon removes part of the stomach and seals the new sleeve with a staple line. Keep it clinical but accessible.

### Risks / honest section
- **Intro paragraph tone:** Acknowledge directly that gastric sleeve surgery is major surgery with real risks. The practice would rather the reader hear these plainly now than be surprised later. The surgeon will go through each risk in the context of the individual's health before any decision is made.
- **Risk bullets to address:**
  1. Staple line leak — a serious potential complication that may require further treatment; ask the surgeon how this risk is monitored and managed.
  2. Reflux or heartburn — can be new or worsened after a sleeve; symptoms may settle for some and persist for others; it may influence the choice of procedure.
  3. Nutritional deficiencies — particularly vitamin B12, iron and vitamin D; lifelong supplements and regular blood tests are part of staying well.
  4. Hair thinning — can occur in the months after surgery and may be associated with rapid weight loss or reduced nutritional intake; patients should discuss persistent symptoms or nutritional concerns with their care team.
  5. Weight regain — possible if eating habits change or follow-up lapses; ongoing clinical review remains part of aftercare.
  6. General surgical risks — bleeding, infection, blood clots and anaesthetic reactions, as with any operation.
