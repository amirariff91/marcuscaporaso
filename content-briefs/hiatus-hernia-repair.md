## Page purpose
This page serves Australian adults living with reflux, GORD or a diagnosed hiatus hernia who want to understand what surgical repair involves and what it costs. The primary keyword is national in demand, so the page is written primarily as a national resource with a clear Perth section — it explains what a hiatus hernia is, when surgery is indicated, the laparoscopic fundoplication approach, recovery and cost, and positions the practice's upper GI surgeons as the people to assess whether repair is right. It is medically relevant to a bariatric practice because hiatus hernia and reflux commonly accompany or follow weight-loss surgery, especially gastric sleeve.

### What is a hiatus hernia, and what does repair involve?
- ~3 paragraphs
- Para 1: Define a hiatus hernia — part of the stomach slides up through the hiatus in the diaphragm. Note many small hernias are harmless; the problem is when the valve weakens and acid refluxes (GORD).
- Para 2: Define the repair — return the stomach below the diaphragm, tighten the opening, and usually add a fundoplication (wrap) to recreate a one-way valve. State the aim is to reduce reflux and relieve symptoms.
- Para 3: Explain the laparoscopic/keyhole approach and its general advantages (less pain, shorter stay, faster recovery) vs open surgery. Note larger/complex hernias can be harder; the surgeon will explain the individual case.

### Comparison / approach note (bariatric crossover)
Short bridging paragraph: hiatus hernia and reflux often overlap with bariatric surgery — a gastric sleeve can worsen reflux, and a hiatus hernia is sometimes found and repaired at the same time as weight-loss surgery. Because the surgeons are upper GI specialists, they assess the whole picture rather than treating reflux and weight separately.

### Eligibility ("is surgery right for you")
- BMI note: There is no BMI threshold — this is a medically indicated upper GI operation. The decision rests on symptoms, investigations and how well other treatments have worked, not a number.
- Bullet points to address:
  - Troublesome reflux/GORD not settled despite medication (PPIs)
  - Persistent regurgitation of food or acid into throat or mouth
  - A larger hernia, or one causing chest discomfort, swallowing or breathing problems
  - Preference for surgery over long-term medication is a reasonable conversation to have
  - Investigations (endoscopy, sometimes pH/pressure studies) confirm diagnosis and guide suitability
  - Surgery is not right for everyone — if medication/lifestyle is the better path, the surgeon will say so honestly

### How it works (6 steps)
Write each step as a heading and 2–3 sentences. Tone is measured and reassuring.

1. **GP Referral** — What a referral is for, how it enables Medicare rebates, why it is the starting point for seeing an upper GI surgeon.
2. **Assessment & Investigations** — Surgeon reviews symptoms; gastroscopy and sometimes pH/oesophageal pressure studies confirm the hernia and its impact.
3. **Honest Consultation** — Patient meets surgeon to discuss whether repair is right, weighing surgery vs continued medication, with likely benefits and risks for their case.
4. **Preparing for Surgery** — Pre-operative instructions including fasting and medication adjustments; surgeon and anaesthetist cover anything specific.
5. **Laparoscopic Repair** — Keyhole repair, usually with fundoplication, under general anaesthetic at SJOG Murdoch or Subiaco.
6. **Recovery & Follow-up** — One to two nights typical; soft/liquid diet for a few weeks while the wrap settles; follow-up reviews.

### Benefits (5 bullets)
No overclaiming — use "many people", "can", "aims to".

- Reduction in reflux and heartburn for many people not controlled by medication
- Relief from regurgitation that disturbs sleep and daily life
- An alternative to long-term reflux medication for those who prefer it
- Repair of the hernia itself, reducing the chance it enlarges over time
- Assessment by upper GI specialists who address reflux and bariatric considerations together

### Risks / honest section
- Intro paragraph: Well-established and generally safe, but all surgery carries risk. Note the fundoplication can cause temporary swallowing difficulty or gas-bloat that usually eases; general surgical risks; possible recurrence. Frame as respect for the reader. Most patients do well; surgeon explains how risks apply individually.
- Risk bullets to address:
  - Temporary difficulty swallowing as the wrap settles, usually improving over weeks
  - Bloating / inability to belch (gas-bloat), tends to ease with time and a gradual diet
  - General surgical risks (bleeding, infection, blood clots, anaesthetic reaction)
  - Small chance the hernia or reflux returns over time, occasionally needing further treatment
  - No guarantee of a complete cure — surgery aims to reduce symptoms; results vary; surgeon will advise

### Cost paragraph
Cover: final figure depends on private health cover, surgeon and anaesthetist fees, length of stay, and any additional procedures. As a medically indicated operation, eligible cases attract a Medicare rebate and a contribution from most private health funds with appropriate hospital cover. Do NOT quote a single figure. State every patient receives a clear, itemised written estimate before any decision.

### Outcomes note
Short honest paragraph: for many, repair significantly reduces reflux and improves comfort, but it is not a guaranteed cure and results vary; some symptoms can persist and a small number of hernias recur. Best outcomes come from the right diagnosis, the right operation and realistic expectations. Where medication/other measures are better, the practice will say so rather than recommend unneeded surgery.

Direction: Meet the reader at their symptom (reflux / hiatus hernia wearing them down). Collaborative, not pushy — invite them to work out together whether repair is the right answer.
