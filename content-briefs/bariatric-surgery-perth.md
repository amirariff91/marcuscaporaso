### What is bariatric surgery?
- ~3 paragraphs
- Para 1: Define bariatric surgery in plain language — procedures that change how the stomach (and sometimes small intestine) handles food; goal is earlier satiety, less hunger, and in some procedures changed absorption. Contextualise it as a medical intervention for severe obesity when diet and exercise have not been enough.
- Para 2: Be honest about what surgery is not — not a quick fix, not something that works alone. Acknowledge that obesity is a chronic medical condition, not a willpower problem. Surgery treats the medical condition. Long-term results depend on what the patient does alongside the procedure.
- Para 3: Explain that most procedures are performed laparoscopically (keyhole), using small incisions. Do not make comparative pain, recovery or safety claims; explain that the surgeon will discuss the technique, expected recovery, alternatives and individual risks. Make clear that procedure choice is never one-size-fits-all — the surgeon advises based on weight, health conditions, eating patterns, and the patient's goals.
- Closing comparison note (below paragraphs): Orient the reader to what they will find next — a plain overview of the four main surgical options so they can walk into their consultation with a sense of what the conversation will cover.

### Eligibility section
- BMI summary to anchor the section: "BMI 40 or above, or BMI 35–39.9 with a major obesity-related condition — always subject to individual clinical assessment."
- Write each of the following points clearly — do not combine or dilute them:
  - BMI 40 or above: MBS eligibility is assessed individually against the applicable item, clinical and referral requirements; BMI alone does not guarantee eligibility
  - BMI 40 or above, or BMI 35–39.9 with a major obesity-related condition — always subject to individual clinical assessment
  - BMI below 35: this does not meet the MBS threshold for these items; discuss any non-MBS clinical options with the surgeon, who will assess suitability individually
  - History of genuine attempts at weight loss through diet, exercise or medical means without lasting success: this is expected and not held against the patient
  - Commitment to long-term dietary changes, supplements where needed, and ongoing follow-up: the surgery is one part; aftercare supports monitoring and ongoing care
  - Final eligibility: always determined after a full assessment with the surgeon and wider team, including medical history review and review of conditions that affect surgical risk

### Procedures section (written as benefit bullets)
This section replaces the generic "benefits" format — each bullet introduces one procedure. Cover all six:

1. **Gastric sleeve (sleeve gastrectomy)** — removes part of the stomach to create a smaller stomach; expected effects and suitability vary, so the surgeon explains the individual risks, benefits and follow-up needs
2. **Gastric bypass (Roux-en-Y)** — changes stomach size and how food passes through the small intestine; it has procedure-specific nutritional monitoring and risks to discuss with the surgeon
3. **Mini gastric bypass (one anastomosis bypass)** — a bypass variation with a single join; the surgeon explains its technical features, risks and whether it suits the individual situation
4. **Gastric band (adjustable band)** — an implanted adjustable band that can be adjusted or removed, but still requires surgery, ongoing reviews and monitoring for band-related complications
5. **Revision surgery** — for patients who had a previous procedure (e.g. older band) and need conversion, repair or a change to a different procedure; risks and follow-up differ by case and are assessed individually
6. **Medical weight management** — a non-surgical pathway supported by medical assessment and structured dietitian-led care; suitability and any medication or active-ingredient/class reference are OPEN — pending TGA/legal decision. If retained, use class/ingredient names only and no brands

### Risks — honest section
- Intro paragraph: Acknowledge every operation carries risk. Explain that laparoscopic techniques use small incisions, without making comparative safety, pain or recovery claims. Commit to giving the reader a frank picture rather than something they find out later.
- Risk bullets to address (one sentence each):
  - General surgical risks: bleeding, infection, blood clots, anaesthetic reactions
  - Procedure-specific risks: leaks at staple or join lines in sleeve and bypass; band slippage and erosion with gastric bands
  - Nutritional: bypass procedures in particular can affect absorption of vitamins and minerals — lifelong supplements and monitoring are part of the deal
  - Eating changes require real adjustment: smaller portions, slower eating, avoiding certain foods — especially in the early months
  - Lifestyle change is genuinely required: surgery does not remove the need for healthy habits; weight regain remains possible and follow-up needs vary
  - The possibility of revision surgery depending on the procedure and how the body responds

### Cost paragraph
Cover: total cost depends on procedure, private health cover, level of excess (out of pocket), hospital and anaesthetic fees; Medicare and private-health benefits may apply when the relevant criteria are met, but coverage and gaps vary. Any range or estimate should be discussed at consultation with an itemised breakdown. Superannuation access may be available only under specific circumstances — verify current rules. Do not quote specific dollar figures.
