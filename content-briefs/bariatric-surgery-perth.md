### What is bariatric surgery?
- ~3 paragraphs
- Para 1: Define bariatric surgery in plain language — procedures that change how the stomach (and sometimes small intestine) handles food; goal is earlier satiety, less hunger, and in some procedures changed absorption. Contextualise it as a medical intervention for severe obesity when diet and exercise have not been enough.
- Para 2: Be honest about what surgery is not — not a quick fix, not something that works alone. Acknowledge that obesity is a chronic medical condition, not a willpower problem. Surgery treats the medical condition. Long-term results depend on what the patient does alongside the procedure.
- Para 3: Explain that most procedures are performed laparoscopically (keyhole), meaning smaller incisions, less pain, faster recovery than older open surgery. Make clear that procedure choice is never one-size-fits-all — the surgeon recommends based on weight, health conditions, eating patterns, and the patient's goals.
- Closing comparison note (below paragraphs): Orient the reader to what they will find next — a plain overview of the four main surgical options so they can walk into their consultation with a sense of what the conversation will cover.

### Eligibility section
- BMI summary to anchor the section: "Bariatric surgery is generally considered for adults with a BMI of 40 or above, or a BMI of 35 or above when an obesity-related health condition is also present. People with a BMI between 30 and 35 may be candidates in specific circumstances, particularly where type 2 diabetes is poorly controlled."
- Write each of the following points clearly — do not combine or dilute them:
  - BMI 40+: surgery is generally an appropriate option to discuss, regardless of other conditions
  - BMI 35–40 with a related condition (type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure, sleep apnoea, joint disease): surgery is commonly considered
  - BMI 30–35: may be considered in select cases — often where diabetes is difficult to manage with medication alone; assessed individually
  - History of genuine attempts at weight loss through diet, exercise or medical means without lasting success: this is expected and not held against the patient
  - Commitment to long-term dietary changes, supplements where needed, and ongoing follow-up: the surgery is one part; aftercare is what protects the result
  - Final eligibility: always determined after a full assessment with the surgeon and wider team, including medical history review and review of conditions that affect surgical risk

### How it works (6 steps)
Keep steps in this order — 1–2 sentences covering the content described:

1. **Initial enquiry and GP referral** — most patients start with a GP referral (helps with Medicare and private health); patients can also come directly and the team will guide them on the referral
2. **Consultation with your surgeon** — discuss history, goals, health; the surgeon talks through the right procedure (sleeve, bypass, band, or medical pathway) honestly, including what it can and cannot do
3. **Assessment with the wider team** — may include physician, dietitian, and other clinicians who confirm surgery is safe and appropriate, and prepare the patient for the changes ahead
4. **Pre-surgery preparation** — typically includes a pre-operative diet to shrink the liver and make surgery safer; practical guidance on what to expect on the day and during early recovery
5. **Your procedure at SJOG Murdoch or Subiaco** — laparoscopic in most cases; short hospital stay; surgeon explains the individual recovery plan
6. **Long-term aftercare** — the part that matters most for lasting results; patient stays connected to the dietitian team and clinical staff over months and years; regular reviews, nutritional monitoring, support as the body and habits adjust

### Procedures section (written as benefit bullets)
This section replaces the generic "benefits" format — each bullet introduces one procedure. Cover all six:

1. **Gastric sleeve (sleeve gastrectomy)** — most common procedure; large portion of stomach removed leaving a slim tube; holds less food and reduces hunger hormones; many people lose around 60–70% of excess weight (results vary); no implant; suits a wide range of patients
2. **Gastric bypass (Roux-en-Y)** — restricts intake and changes absorption; typically greater weight loss, often around 70–80% of excess weight (individual results vary); frequently preferred for type 2 diabetes due to its effect on blood sugar; requires lifelong vitamin and mineral monitoring
3. **Mini gastric bypass (one anastomosis bypass)** — variation of bypass with a single join; technically simpler, shorter surgery time; still delivers substantial weight loss for many patients; surgeon advises whether it suits the individual situation
4. **Gastric band (adjustable band)** — most reversible option; no cutting or stapling; can be removed or adjusted; generally produces the least weight loss of the surgical options; requires ongoing adjustments
5. **Revision surgery** — for patients who had a previous procedure (e.g. older band) and need conversion, repair or a change to a different procedure; more complex than first-time surgery; assessed individually
6. **Medical weight management** — non-surgical option; GLP-1 medications and structured dietitian-led programs; suits people who do not want surgery, are not yet candidates, or want support before or instead of an operation

### Risks — honest section
- Intro paragraph: Acknowledge every operation carries risk. Frame it positively: modern keyhole techniques and experienced surgeons make these procedures safe for most suitable candidates. Commit to giving the reader a frank picture rather than something they find out later.
- Risk bullets to address (one sentence each):
  - General surgical risks: bleeding, infection, blood clots, anaesthetic reactions
  - Procedure-specific risks: leaks at staple or join lines in sleeve and bypass; band slippage and erosion with gastric bands
  - Nutritional: bypass procedures in particular can affect absorption of vitamins and minerals — lifelong supplements and monitoring are part of the deal
  - Eating changes require real adjustment: smaller portions, slower eating, avoiding certain foods — especially in the early months
  - Lifestyle change is genuinely required: surgery makes weight loss achievable, but does not remove the need for healthy habits; weight regain is possible if habits slip
  - The possibility of revision surgery depending on the procedure and how the body responds

### Cost paragraph
Cover: total cost depends on procedure, private health cover, level of excess (out of pocket) and hospital and anaesthetic fees; many patients with appropriate private health cover find a significant portion is covered; some access superannuation; the team provides a clear itemised breakdown before any commitment is made, with no pressure or surprises. Do not quote specific dollar figures.
