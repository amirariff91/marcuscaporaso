### How weight loss surgery costs are built (4 paragraphs)

**Paragraph 1 — No sticker price**
Explain that there is no single price and that anyone quoting one figure without knowing the individual's situation is guessing. The total is made up of several separate fees, and how much of each is covered depends on private health insurance, Medicare entitlement and the procedure chosen.

**Paragraph 2 — The fee components**
Name all the components: surgeon's fee, anaesthetist's fee, assistant surgeon's fee (where applicable), and the hospital fee. Note that there can also be costs for the dietitian, follow-up appointments and pre-operative tests. Each is billed separately — this is why the headline number can feel confusing.

**Paragraph 3 — Approximate ranges by procedure**
Provide the approximate planning figures: gastric sleeve typically $12,000–$20,000 out of pocket; gastric bypass typically $15,000–$25,000; gastric banding generally at the lower end. State clearly that these are approximate ranges to help with planning, not quotes — the figure that matters is the personalised estimate the practice prepares.

**Paragraph 4 — The biggest single cost factor**
Explain that the biggest determinant of what the patient actually pays is whether they have appropriate private health insurance and whether the waiting period has been served. With the right cover, the hospital fee is largely covered and out-of-pocket drops considerably. Without it, the hospital component becomes a significant self-funded cost.

### Comparison note (inline callout)
Short, honest note: every range on this page uses "approximately" or "around" deliberately. Costs move with the procedure, the surgeon's assessment, the insurer's policy and the hospital. The practice does not ask patients to make decisions on a ballpark — a written estimate comes first.

### Eligibility and funding suitability
This section must be thorough because it covers several interacting factors. Write it as a checklist-style guide covering:
- **Medicare eligibility:** the MBS bariatric items apply to clinically severe obesity — BMI 40+, or 35+ with a major obesity-related condition. State this SEPARATELY from the practice's own clinical criterion of BMI 35+ or 30+ with an obesity-related health condition. Eligibility is assessed by the surgeon, not assumed.
- **Private health cover — what to check:** Patients generally need gold-tier or equivalent hospital cover that explicitly includes the obesity surgery item numbers. Identify the key Medicare item numbers: 31575 (sleeve) and 31572 (bypass). Stress the difference between a general "weight management" category and the specific item numbers — check the specific items.
- **The 12-month waiting period:** Most funds apply this for obesity surgery as a pre-existing condition. If the patient does not yet have cover, planning around the waiting period is often the most cost-effective single decision they can make.
- **Medicare and the hospital admission:** Medicare provides a rebate on surgical and consultation fees for eligible patients but does NOT cover the hospital admission for elective bariatric surgery. That cost is either covered by private health or paid out-of-pocket.
- **Superannuation:** May be accessible on compassionate grounds where obesity is causing a serious medical condition. Assessed case by case by the regulator — not automatic.
- **When cost is the barrier:** Encourage early conversation with the team — there are often more options than patients expect once they sit down with the numbers.

### How it works (5 steps)
These steps cover both the clinical and financial pathway — the cost story runs through each one.

1. **Initial consultation and clinical assessment** — Surgeon assesses whether surgery is appropriate and which procedure suits the patient; the procedure recommended shapes the cost, so this comes first; consultation attracts a Medicare rebate for eligible patients
2. **Private health cover check** — The team helps confirm whether the patient's policy covers the relevant item numbers (31575, 31572) and whether the 12-month waiting period has been served; this is where the largest cost question is answered
3. **Written, itemised estimate** — Before any commitment, a personalised estimate lists the surgeon, anaesthetist, assistant and hospital fees, what the insurer is expected to cover, and the likely out-of-pocket; no surprises is the practice standard
4. **Funding options discussion** — If there is a gap, the team walks through how Medicare rebates, early super access on compassionate grounds, and third-party payment plans might apply
5. **Decision with full visibility** — Patient proceeds only once every number on the page is understood; this should never feel rushed

### Cost factors and common pitfalls (risks section)
**Tone:** Matter-of-fact and genuinely helpful. Most cost surprises come from gaps in understanding, not from the surgery itself. Frame this as consumer education.

**Opening paragraph:** State that most cost surprises in bariatric surgery arise from misunderstandings rather than from the surgery itself. Knowing these in advance helps avoid them. The practice raises them openly because the alternative is an unwelcome bill after the fact.

**Pitfall bullet points (6 items):**
- Assuming all private health policies cover obesity surgery — many do not; some cover consultations but not the hospital item numbers; always check 31575 and 31572 specifically
- Overlooking the 12-month waiting period — booking before it is served can mean the entire hospital fee falls on the patient
- Forgetting that Medicare does not cover the hospital admission — the Medicare rebate applies to surgical and consultation fees, not the hospital stay
- Not budgeting for separate anaesthetist and assistant surgeon fees, which are billed independently
- Assuming super is automatically available — early release on compassionate grounds is assessed individually and is not guaranteed
- Treating a verbal ballpark as a quote — the only figure to plan around is the written, itemised estimate
