### What is weight loss surgery — and why it is only part of the picture
- Section title: "What we mean by weight loss surgery, and why it is only part of the picture"
- ~4 paragraphs (this page warrants a slightly longer treatment given its breadth)
- Para 1: Define weight loss surgery / bariatric surgery in plain terms — procedures that change stomach size or function to help the patient eat less and feel full sooner. Name the most common procedures: gastric sleeve, gastric bypass, adjustable gastric band. State that these procedures have been used in Australia for many years, while likely benefits, risks and suitability vary by person.
- Para 2: Establish clearly that surgery is not the only road, and not always the first. Explain that obesity is a medical condition, not a willpower failure — this framing matters because it shapes what good treatment looks like. The right answer might be medication, a structured very low calorie diet, dietitian support, an endoscopic option, or surgery — often in combination over time.
- Para 3: Introduce the team composition — not just surgeons; also physicians who manage the medical side of weight, and specialist dietitians whose support is central to the care plan. Whoever the patient sees first, they are stepping into a team that can move them along the spectrum of care as their needs change.
- Para 4: State the aim of the page plainly — to help the reader understand the full range of options so they can make an informed decision, rather than feeling pushed toward any single one.
- Closing comparison note (below paragraphs): Give the reader a quick mental model of the spectrum: medical management (GLP-1 medication, very low calorie diet, dietitian support) at one end, surgical procedures (sleeve, bypass, band) at the other. Acknowledge that many people move between them over time, and there is no single correct starting point.

### Eligibility section
- BMI summary to anchor the section: "You do not need a very high BMI to start a conversation. A BMI of 27 or above, particularly where a weight-related health condition is present, is a reason to request an individual medical assessment for non-surgical options. For the Medicare-listed surgical pathway, current MBS thresholds refer to BMI 40+, or BMI 35–39.9 with a major co-morbidity. We will help you understand what needs to be assessed."
- Write each of the following points — keep them accessible and non-clinical:
  - BMI around 27+ with a condition such as type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure, or sleep apnoea: invite an individual medical assessment for non-surgical options
  - Current MBS surgical thresholds: BMI 40+, or BMI 35–39.9 with a major co-morbidity; clinical suitability is assessed individually
  - Higher BMI with prior attempts at weight loss that did not hold: surgical and non-surgical options can be assessed together
  - Readiness to engage with ongoing support (dietitian appointments) as part of any pathway
  - Age, general health, previous surgery, and current medications are all considered at assessment
  - If the reader is unsure what can be assessed: that uncertainty is exactly what an initial consultation is for — explain the options without promising eligibility

### How it works (5 steps)
Keep steps in this order — 1–2 sentences covering the content described:

1. **An honest first consultation** — the team listens to history, prior attempts, health, and what the patient actually wants; no judgement; this conversation shapes everything that follows
2. **Assessment by the right team member** — depending on the situation, this may be a physician, surgeon, or dietitian first; patients are matched to the part of the team that fits where they are starting from
3. **A pathway proposed, not prescribed** — the team lays out realistic options from medical management through to surgery, explains likely benefits and trade-offs of each; the patient decides together with the team
4. **Treatment, with support built in** — whether the plan involves medication, a very low calorie diet, an endoscopic option, or an operation, the dietitians and clinical team stay involved throughout
5. **Long-term follow-up** — weight management is ongoing; the team reviews progress, adjusts the plan as needs change, and provides follow-up over time

### Risks — honest section
- Section title: "Being honest about the trade-offs"
- Intro paragraph: Acknowledge that every option on the spectrum carries its own considerations. Frame this as a genuine service to the reader — the team will not pretend otherwise. Medications have side effects. Very low calorie diets need supervision. Surgery is a significant step with real risks and recovery. The clinician should explain how these considerations relate to the reader's medical situation before any commitment.
- Risk bullets to address (one sentence each):
  - GLP-1 medications can cause nausea, digestive symptoms and other side effects; not suitable for everyone
  - Very low calorie diets require supervision and are not designed as a permanent eating pattern
  - Surgical procedures carry risks including bleeding, infection, leaks, blood clots and anaesthetic reactions
  - After surgery, lifelong changes to eating and, in some cases, vitamin supplementation are needed
  - Outcomes vary, and weight regain can occur; ongoing follow-up remains part of care
  - The best option for someone else may not be right for this reader — individual assessment matters

### Cost paragraph
Cover: cost depends entirely on which pathway is chosen — medical management and dietitian support look very different in cost to surgery; within surgery the procedure type matters too; private health cover, Medicare rebates, and in some cases superannuation access can all play a part; any applicable estimate or range should be discussed at consultation for the specific situation. Do not quote specific dollar figures.

## Tone and compliance notes
- Medical advertising: no guarantees, use phrases like "clinical averages", "results vary", "your clinician will advise", "many people experience"
- AHPRA/TGA: no testimonials, no before/after claims, no guaranteed outcomes
- MBS surgical threshold: state BMI 40+, or BMI 35–39.9 with a major co-morbidity as assessment guidance; BMI below 35 does not meet that MBS threshold. Do not present a lower-BMI statement as surgical eligibility.
- GLP-1/medical-weight-management advertising policy is pending: use active-ingredient/class names only (semaglutide, liraglutide or GLP-1), never brand names, and verify the final TGA/legal position before publication
- Voice: plain-language, empathetic, direct; avoid hype; this page is the broadest entry point and the reader may be at their most uncertain and guarded
- Reader: a Perth adult at the beginning of their research, possibly BMI 27–35, may not know the word "bariatric", may feel shame or exhaustion — the page must normalise the conversation before it can sell it
- This page should do the most to establish "obesity as a medical condition, not a willpower failure" — that framing is the emotional foundation the rest of the site is built on
- Do not over-index on surgery; the medical management pathway is genuinely presented as an equal option on this page
