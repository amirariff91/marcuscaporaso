### What is a gastric bypass?
- ~3 paragraphs
- **Para 1:** Define the Roux-en-Y procedure in plain terms. The surgeon creates a small stomach pouch (roughly the size of an egg) and connects it directly to a lower section of the small intestine. Food travels a shorter path, so the patient feels full sooner and the body absorbs fewer kilojoules.
- **Para 2:** Explain the dual mechanism — volume restriction (smaller pouch) and hormonal change (gut hormones controlling hunger, fullness and blood sugar are altered by the rerouting). This is the reason the bypass has a strong evidence base for type 2 diabetes management. Many patients see blood sugar improvement within days of surgery, often before significant weight loss occurs — qualify clearly that the extent and duration of improvement varies between individuals.
- **Para 3:** Note that the operation is performed keyhole (laparoscopic) through small incisions — less pain post-operatively, smaller scars, quicker recovery than open surgery. Add that the surgeon confirms keyhole suitability at consultation.

**Comparison note to weave in:** Compared to a gastric sleeve, the bypass tends to give slightly more weight loss and stronger results for type 2 diabetes and acid reflux, but it is a more involved operation with lifelong vitamin needs. The right choice is made with the surgeon based on the individual's health and goals.

### Eligibility
- **BMI note:** Generally suitable for patients with a BMI of 35 or above, or BMI 30 or above with related health conditions.
- **Bullet points to address:**
  1. Often recommended for people living with type 2 diabetes, given the hormonal changes that can improve blood sugar control.
  2. May suit patients with significant acid reflux, where a gastric sleeve might worsen symptoms.
  3. The patient must be ready to commit to lifelong dietary changes and daily vitamin and mineral supplements after surgery.
  4. Eligibility is always a clinical decision made with the patient — BMI is a starting point, not the whole picture. Overall health, history of weight-loss attempts and personal goals all matter.

### How it works (6 steps)
Each step gets a short heading and 1–2 sentences of content direction.

1. **GP referral** — The reader asks their GP for a referral to the practice. A referral enables Medicare rebates on consultations and is the first formal step toward seeing a surgeon.
2. **Orientation consultation** — The reader meets their surgeon to discuss health, goals and whether a bypass is the right fit. No pressure to decide on the day. Frame this as an open conversation, not a sales meeting.
3. **Pre-operative assessment** — The patient sees dietitians and physicians to prepare the body and plan nutrition. Some patients follow a short pre-surgery eating plan to make the operation safer. This is team-led support, not a handout.
4. **Surgery** — Keyhole procedure, approximately two hours under general anaesthetic, at St John of God Murdoch or Subiaco. Keep language clinical and calm.
5. **Hospital recovery** — Typically 3–4 nights. The care team manages comfort, starts the patient on fluids, and monitors movement and recovery. Longer stay than sleeve reflects the complexity of the operation.
6. **Ongoing support** — Regular follow-up with surgeon and dietitian well beyond the operation. Position this as a long-term relationship, not a transaction. If weight loss stalls, the team reviews and adjusts rather than leaving the patient on their own.

### Benefits (5 bullets)
Each bullet conveys one clear, qualified benefit. No superlatives.

1. Significant, durable weight loss — clinical averages around 70–80% of excess weight, though individual results vary.
2. Strong improvement in type 2 diabetes in many patients — some are able to reduce or stop medication after discussion with their doctor; outcomes vary between individuals.
3. Often relieves acid reflux and heartburn, unlike some procedures that can worsen it.
4. Improvement in conditions linked to excess weight — high blood pressure, sleep apnoea, joint pain.
5. Long-term hormonal changes that reduce constant hunger, making healthier eating feel more achievable rather than a daily battle.

### Risks / honest section
- **Intro paragraph tone:** Acknowledge directly that the bypass is safe and well-established in experienced hands, but that no surgery is without risk. The patient deserves the full picture before deciding. Most patients recover without serious complications, but the points below are what every patient is told before proceeding. The surgeon will discuss how each applies individually.
- **Risk bullets to address:**
  1. General surgical risks — bleeding, infection, blood clots, reaction to anaesthetic.
  2. Small risk of a leak at one of the new surgical connections — monitored closely during the hospital stay.
  3. Lifelong need for vitamin and mineral supplements, because the rerouting reduces nutrient absorption.
  4. Dumping syndrome — feeling unwell after eating sugary or fatty foods; usually improves as eating habits adjust.
