### What is a gastric bypass?
- ~3 paragraphs
- **Para 1:** Define the Roux-en-Y procedure in plain terms. The surgeon creates a small stomach pouch (roughly the size of an egg) and connects it directly to a lower section of the small intestine. Food travels a shorter path, so the patient feels full sooner and the body absorbs fewer kilojoules.
- **Para 2:** Explain the dual mechanism — volume restriction (smaller pouch) and hormonal change (gut hormones controlling hunger, fullness and blood sugar are altered by the rerouting). This contributes to the evidence base for type 2 diabetes management. Some patients may see blood sugar changes early after surgery, but the extent, timing and duration of improvement vary between individuals.
- **Para 3:** Note that the operation may be performed keyhole (laparoscopic) through small incisions. Wound size, pain, scarring, recovery time and complications vary; the surgeon confirms whether a keyhole approach is appropriate at consultation.

**Comparison note to weave in:** Compared with a gastric sleeve, gastric bypass may be discussed for some patients with type 2 diabetes or significant reflux, but it is a more involved operation with lifelong vitamin needs. The right choice is made with the surgeon based on the individual's health, goals and risk profile.

### Eligibility
- **BMI note:** Eligibility is assessed individually. BMI 40 or above, or BMI 35–39.9 with a major obesity-related condition — always subject to individual clinical assessment.
- **Bullet points to address:**
  1. May be discussed for people living with type 2 diabetes, given the hormonal changes that can affect blood sugar control; this does not establish eligibility.
  2. May be relevant for patients with significant acid reflux, where a gastric sleeve might worsen symptoms for some people; this does not establish eligibility.
  3. The patient must be ready to commit to lifelong dietary changes and daily vitamin and mineral supplements after surgery.
  4. Eligibility is always a clinical decision made with the patient — BMI is a starting point, not the whole picture. Overall health, history of weight-loss attempts and personal goals all matter.

1. **GP referral** — The reader asks their GP for a referral to the practice. A referral may allow a Medicare rebate for an eligible consultation, subject to Medicare rules, and is the first formal step toward seeing a surgeon.
2. **Orientation consultation** — The reader meets their surgeon to discuss health, goals and whether a bypass is the right fit. No pressure to decide on the day. Frame this as an open conversation, not a sales meeting.
3. **Pre-operative assessment** — The patient sees dietitians and physicians to prepare for surgery and plan nutrition. A pre-surgery eating plan may be prescribed to reduce liver size; its purpose, duration and risks are explained individually. This is team-led support, not a handout.

### Risks / honest section
- **Intro paragraph tone:** Acknowledge directly that the bypass is a major operation and no surgery is without risk. Give the patient the full picture before deciding, including serious complications that can occur, and explain that the surgeon will discuss how each risk applies individually.
- **Risk bullets to address:**
  1. General surgical risks — bleeding, infection, blood clots, reaction to anaesthetic.
  2. Risk of a leak at one of the new surgical connections — monitored closely during the hospital stay.
  3. Lifelong need for vitamin and mineral supplements, because the rerouting reduces nutrient absorption.
  4. Dumping syndrome — feeling unwell after eating sugary or fatty foods; symptoms may change as eating habits adjust. Your dietitian will explain what to watch for and when to seek advice.
