### What is a gastric band?
- ~3 paragraphs
- Para 1: Describe what the band is physically — a soft silicone ring around the upper stomach creating a small pouch. Emphasise what makes it different from every other procedure: nothing is cut, stapled or removed.
- Para 2: Explain how the band is adjustable — the port under the skin, the saline injection process, and why this means ongoing follow-up is built into the treatment rather than optional.
- Para 3: Explain the trade-off: no change to gut hormones or intestine means slower, smaller weight loss than a sleeve or bypass. Frame this honestly as the reason some patients specifically want it — the reversibility is the point.

### Comparison note
Include a short bridging paragraph (not a full section) acknowledging the band gives less weight loss and slower results than the other procedures, and that it requires regular visits to function. Then flip it: what it offers in return is reversibility and the least invasive operation. Direct the reader toward the consultation as the right place to weigh this up.

### Eligibility
- BMI note: Often considered for patients with a BMI between 30 and 40, particularly where reversibility is a priority.
- Bullet points to address:
  - Suits patients who specifically want a reversible option and are comfortable with gradual weight loss
  - Can be appropriate at a lower BMI (30–40 range) where a sleeve or bypass may be more than is needed
  - Generally not recommended if the patient has significant acid reflux, as the band can worsen heartburn
  - Only works well with commitment to regular adjustment visits — frame it as a partnership, not a set-and-forget procedure
  - Eligibility is always a clinical decision; BMI is not the only factor — overall health, goals and readiness for follow-up all matter

### How it works (6 steps)
Write each step as a short heading and 2–3 sentences of explanation. Tone is reassuring, process-forward.

1. **GP Referral** — What a referral is for, how it enables Medicare rebates, why it's the starting point.
2. **Orientation Consultation** — Meeting the surgeon, discussing health and goals, understanding if the band is the right fit. Note: no pressure to decide on the day.
3. **Pre-operative Assessment** — Seeing dietitians and physicians, preparing the body, possible short pre-surgery eating plan.
4. **Surgery** — Keyhole procedure under general anaesthetic at SJOG Murdoch or Subiaco; the band is placed around the upper stomach and the port is positioned under the skin.
5. **Hospital Recovery** — Typically one night in hospital; team manages comfort, starts fluids, checks recovery before discharge.
6. **Adjustments and Ongoing Support** — Regular saline adjustment visits over following months, plus surgeon and dietitian follow-up to sustain results.

### Benefits (6 bullets)
Each bullet should communicate one clear advantage. Avoid hype; frame factually.

- The only weight-loss procedure that is fully reversible — band can be removed and stomach returns to normal shape
- Nothing is cut, stapled or removed; the least invasive option of all the procedures offered
- Adjustable over time — can be tightened or loosened as the patient's needs change
- Shorter hospital stay and quicker initial recovery than a sleeve or bypass
- No long-term requirement for the vitamin and mineral supplements that a bypass calls for, since absorption is unaffected
- Gradual, steady weight loss for patients who are comfortable with a slower pace — clinical averages around 40–50% of excess weight, individual results vary

### Risks / honest section
- Intro paragraph: Set the tone immediately — the patient deserves the full picture before deciding. With the band, this means being clear about both limitations (less weight loss, slower results, dependence on follow-up) and device-specific risks. Most patients do well, but these are the points every band patient should understand. Close by pointing to the consultation where the surgeon will discuss how these apply individually.
- Risk bullets to address:
  - Band slippage — band moves out of position; may need a further procedure to correct
  - Erosion — band gradually works into the stomach wall over time; usually requires removal
  - Port problems — infection, leaking, or port moving under the skin
  - The need for ongoing adjustment visits — without them, the band may give little weight loss
  - Less weight loss on average than a sleeve or bypass, and a slower rate of loss
  - General surgical risks: bleeding, infection, blood clots, anaesthetic reaction

### Cost paragraph
Cover: the total cost depends on private health cover, surgeon and anaesthetist fees, hospital stay, and the ongoing adjustment visits the band requires. Note the Medicare item number (31569). Explain that Medicare and most private health funds with appropriate cover contribute toward bariatric surgery, and that some patients use superannuation early release for out-of-pocket costs. Do not quote a single figure. State that every patient receives a clear, itemised written estimate before any decision is made.

### Outcomes note
This is a short paragraph of honest expectation-setting. State that the band does not give the largest weight loss available, and that results depend heavily on attending adjustments and making lifestyle changes alongside it. Acknowledge that some patients later convert to a sleeve for more weight loss, and that this pathway is available. Outcomes vary between individuals; the practice's role is honest advice, the right procedure, and support for a realistic result.
