### What is revision bariatric surgery?
- ~3 paragraphs
- Para 1: Define revision bariatric surgery — a second weight-loss operation after a first has stopped working or caused problems. Clarify it is not a single operation but a group of them, and the right one depends entirely on what was done the first time and what has gone wrong.
- Para 2: Describe the most common revision patterns the practice sees: sleeve converted to bypass (usually for severe reflux or weight regain), band removed and converted to sleeve or bypass (slippage, erosion, or failure), and bypass revision (weight regain or complication). Keep this descriptive, not exhaustive.
- Para 3: State clearly and without softening: revision surgery is technically more demanding than a first operation. Previous surgery leaves scar tissue and altered anatomy. This is why careful planning and an experienced surgeon matter so much — it is not said to frighten but to set accurate expectations.

### Comparison / approach note
Include a short bridging paragraph: unlike a first procedure, revision surgery always starts by asking why the first one didn't last. Two people with the same regained weight can need completely different revisions. The assessment is the most important part of the whole process.

### Eligibility
- BMI note: There is no single BMI cut-off for revision surgery. Suitability depends far more on what the first operation was, what has gone wrong, and overall health than on a number alone.
- Bullet points to address:
  - Weight has returned after an earlier sleeve, band or bypass, and lifestyle changes alone have not held the loss
  - Severe acid reflux after a gastric sleeve — conversion to bypass is a common and well-understood response
  - A band that has slipped, eroded, caused swallowing problems or simply failed
  - The practice looks closely at why the first surgery did not deliver — revision rarely succeeds if the underlying cause is not understood and addressed
  - A strong, realistic commitment to post-operative diet, supplements and follow-up matters even more the second time
  - Revision is not right for everyone — if the practice does not believe it is the best path, they will say so honestly

### How it works (6 steps)
Write each step as a heading and 2–3 sentences. Tone is measured and thorough — this is a more complex process than a first procedure and the content should reflect that.

1. **GP Referral** — What a referral is for, how it enables Medicare rebates, why it is the starting point for seeing a surgeon about revision.
2. **Reviewing Your First Operation** — The practice gathers details of the original surgery including operation reports and imaging. Knowing exactly what was done is essential before planning a revision.
3. **Working Out What Went Wrong** — Through consultation and investigations (imaging, endoscopy), the surgeon establishes why the first procedure did not last. This step shapes everything that follows.
4. **Honest Consultation** — The patient meets the surgeon to discuss realistic options and expectations. Revision is harder and carries more risk; the patient needs to understand this fully before any decision is made.
5. **Surgery** — Performed at SJOG Murdoch or Subiaco. Often a longer, more complex operation than the first procedure. Explain this without alarm — it is a statement of surgical reality.
6. **Ongoing Support** — Recovery and long-term success depend on follow-up. Regular reviews with surgeon and dietitian, with structured support aimed at making this result the one that lasts.

### Benefits (5 bullets)
Each bullet addresses a specific, realistic benefit. No overclaiming.

- A chance to address returned weight when the underlying cause is understood and treatable
- Relief from severe acid reflux — a frequent reason for converting a gastric sleeve to a bypass
- Resolution of band-related problems (slippage, erosion, swallowing difficulty) by removing the band and converting to another procedure
- Renewed improvement in weight-related conditions such as type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure and sleep apnoea (note: discuss with your doctor)
- A fresh, honest plan built around what did not work last time, rather than repeating the same approach

### Risks / honest section
- Intro paragraph: Be direct immediately — revision surgery is harder and carries higher risk than a first-time operation. Scar tissue and altered anatomy make the procedure more technically difficult, and that raises the chance of complications. Frame this as respect for the reader, not as a warning designed to scare. Most patients recover well; the surgeon will talk through exactly how these risks apply to the individual.
- Risk bullets to address:
  - Higher risk of complications than first-time surgery — scar tissue and altered anatomy make the operation more demanding
  - Increased risk of leaks at surgical connections — careful monitoring in hospital
  - General surgical risks (bleeding, infection, blood clots, anaesthetic reaction) which can be greater than the first time
  - Often a longer operation and a longer recovery than the original procedure
  - No guarantee of success — revision can help but does not always achieve the hoped-for result, particularly if the original cause is not addressed

### Cost paragraph
Cover: revision is often more involved than a first procedure, which can affect total cost. Final figure depends on private health cover, surgeon and anaesthetist fees, and hospital stay. Medicare and most private health funds with appropriate cover contribute toward eligible bariatric surgery; some patients access superannuation early release for out-of-pocket costs. Do not quote a single figure. State that every patient receives a clear, itemised written estimate before any decision is made.

### Outcomes note
A short honest paragraph: revision can be effective when the original cause is understood and addressed, and when the patient is committed to the lifestyle, eating and follow-up that come after. Outcomes are less predictable than first-time procedures, and results vary between individuals. The practice would rather give a realistic picture than an empty promise. Where they do not believe revision is in the patient's best interest, they will say so — and they would rather have that honest conversation than set someone up for another disappointment.

## Tone and compliance notes
- Medical advertising: no guarantees, use phrases like "clinical averages", "results vary", "your surgeon will advise"; this page is especially sensitive because the reader has already been through a procedure and may feel let down
- AHPRA/TGA: no testimonials, no before/after claims, no guaranteed outcomes
- Voice: plain-language, empathetic, direct; avoid hype; the page earns trust by being unusually honest about risk
- Reader: a Perth adult who has already had bariatric surgery; they feel some mix of frustration, shame and anxiety; they want to know if there is a path forward and whether they will be judged for needing a second operation — the page must never, at any point, make them feel like a failure
- Critical balance: the page must be honest about the higher risks of revision without being so cautious it feels like a rebuff; the goal is informed decision-making, not dissuasion
